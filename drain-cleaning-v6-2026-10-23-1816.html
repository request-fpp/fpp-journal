H2: The Washing Machine Standpipe and the Dishwasher Line

The washer drains into a standpipe in the wall, and the trap at the bottom of it collects what the washer sends: lint, hair, threads, the dirt out of the clothes. The line slows for a while, and then one day it stops. You start a load, leave the house, and the water comes back up the standpipe and out of the outlet box in the wall. Not the most common call, but a regular one. The standpipe trap gets cleared like any other trap.

The dishwasher is a different story, and it's about how it was connected. The right way is a drain hose to an air gap on the countertop, and from the air gap to the garbage disposal. When the line from the air gap to the disposal clogs, the water comes out at the air gap, and the cap on it sends most of it into the sink. That's a small job: the short line from the air gap to the disposal comes off and gets cleared. The second way, without an air gap, is a high loop: the drain hose runs up as high as it goes under the counter before it drops to the disposal, so dirty sink water can't run back into the dishwasher. The third way we see a lot, and it's the worst: the hose straight into the disposal, no air gap, no loop. Every time the disposal backs up a little, the dirty water goes back into the dishwasher. In all three the clog sits at the connection to the disposal. A disposal that hums or leaks under the sink is garbage disposal repair.

H2: Newer Houses and Older Houses: PVC, Slope and Cast Iron

A newer house with PVC drain lines is the easy case. The pipe is smooth, the fall the plumber set is still there, and there isn't much on the walls yet. Those lines clog because of what went down them, and they clear and stay clear. As the house ages, even PVC changes. The ground under the slab moves, the foundation settles, an inch here and two there, nothing you see in the walls, and a drain line that was set with a fall toward the main loses it. Water that used to run now crawls, and what's in it settles and builds. That's how a line in a house with no visible problem starts clogging again and again.

Cast iron is the hard case. In the Plano houses from the 70s and 80s the drains under the floor are cast iron, 30 to 50 years old, rusted and scaled inside, pinched where it shifted, cracked in places. Every rough spot catches what goes by. Toilet paper is made to fall apart in water. Wet wipes are not, and in a cast iron line the rust and the sharp turns catch them and start the next stoppage.

H2: Drain Cleaning FAQ

Why is my bathroom sink filling up when nobody is using it? The air conditioner is draining into that sink line, and the line is clogged. The AC makes up to about 20 gallons of water a day in summer, and when the sink line can't take it, the water comes up in the sink and over the counter. Shut the AC off at the thermostat and the water stops. Then the line gets cleared with the chain snake. In the older houses that line sometimes runs into the tub overflow instead of the sink, and the overflow is where it gets cleared.

The toilet downstairs comes up when we shower upstairs. Which one is clogged? Neither. The main line is blocked, and everything from upstairs is coming out at the lowest open drain in the house. Stop using water everywhere, the washer and the dishwasher too, and take the cap off the outside cleanout if you can find it, so the standing water runs out onto the ground. We clear the main line through that cleanout with the chain snake, and the camera goes in to show why it clogged.

Do you put a camera in the line when you clear a clogged drain? On every main line and on every branch line we open through a toilet, yes, and it's part of the clearing, not an extra. A bathroom sink line is often too narrow for the camera. On a main line the camera is the whole point: roots at a joint, a coupling that came apart, a settled section, or nothing wrong at all. You see the screen with the plumber.

Does liquid drain cleaner fix a slow shower? Once, maybe. The chemical eats the hair and leaves a sludge in the trap that doesn't rinse out, and over a year or two of pouring more in, that sludge hardens like calcium. We've opened 2 inch shower lines closed down to an opening smaller than a finger after years of it. The chain snake breaks that out. A bottle doesn't.

My kitchen sink was snaked two weeks ago, and it's clogged again. Why? A short cable opened a hole through the grease, and the grease on the pipe wall closed it back up.
