H2: What a Pressure Reducing Valve Does

A PRV takes the city's pressure on one side and gives the house a steady, lower pressure on the other. That's the whole job. The plumbing code caps the pressure inside a house at 80 PSI and requires a PRV wherever the city sends more, and here the city sends more. The people who make faucets, water heaters, dishwashers and washing machines rate them for 80 PSI too. Above that, every joint, every valve, every rubber seal and the water heater itself carries more pressure than it was built for. Below about 40 the house feels it the other way: a thin stream in the shower.

We set a new valve to about 70 PSI. It's the middle we like: well under 80, so the system isn't under stress, and well over 40, so nobody stands under a trickle. In parts of McKinney and Frisco the city's own line runs at 50 to 60 PSI, the city has come out and measured it, and there a new PRV gives you what the main gives it.

The valve is the homeowner's. The city's responsibility ends at the meter; what the pressure does inside the house is yours to check, and a gauge on an outside spigot once a year is the check that catches a valve before it fails.

H2: A Water Pressure Test First: the Gauge on the Spigot

A pressure gauge screws onto an outside spigot or onto the washing machine bib. With no water running in the house it shows the static pressure. Over 80 is a problem whether anything feels wrong or not, and at 100 nothing feels wrong at all. Then open a tap inside and watch the needle. A healthy system drops a little and holds. A clogged PRV holds the number while nothing runs and gives it up the moment something does.

That is the test our plumber runs before anything else on a pressure call, and it's the test we ask you to run once a year. On a house that reads fine, you've spent two minutes. On a house at 110, you've found the reason the toilet fill valves keep failing.

H2: Signs a Pressure Reducing Valve Is Failing

Three things, and each one is a different failure inside the same valve.

The first and the most common: the pressure drops when you use water. The gauge reads fine, even high, with everything closed. Open the shower, and for the first twenty or thirty seconds it runs like it should, because that's the water that was sitting in the pipes under pressure. Then it falls off. Open a second tap and it falls off more. That valve is clogged. Hard water has filled it with scale, and it passes pressure but not volume.

The second is the opposite: too much pressure. Water sprays out of every faucet, the toilet fill valves stop shutting off and the tank keeps running, and when you close a tap you hear the pipe bang in the wall. That bang is water hammer, and it's a PRV that has stopped holding the city back.

The third is plain: the valve leaks in its box. You see a wet spot in the flower bed, or the city sends a notice about water use, or you lift the lid once and the box is full of water. The part people don't think about until it happens: the house's own shut-off valve sits in that same box. A valve that has stood in water for a year is rusted when you need it: the night a pipe goes and you run out to close the house, the handle comes off in your hand.

H2: How Long Does a Pressure Reducing Valve Last?

The usual figure is ten to fifteen years, and that's about right for a valve in a dry box with gravel under it. Here we see them fail from five. Two things shorten the life. The first is the box. A PRV is meant to sit in an accessible valve box with gravel on the bottom, so that rain and the sprinklers drain out instead of standing around the brass. When the builder buried it, or the ground moved and the box silted over, the valve sits in wet dirt and corrodes from the outside while the water scales it from the inside. The second is the water itself: hard, and it clogs the valve the way it clogs a shower head.

Most of the valves we replace are the builder's original. The house is ten years old or more, nobody has opened the box since the day it was set, and the first anybody hears of the PRV is the day it quits.

H2: Can a PRV Be Adjusted, or Does It Need Replacement?

A new PRV is adjusted, that's how it gets set: a bolt on top, turned until the gauge on the spigot reads 70. An old one is not. That's a rule we made the hard way, years ago, and we don't break it. H2: The Expansion Tank: What a PRV Does to the Water Heater

A PRV is also a one way valve. Water comes in and nothing goes back out to the city. That makes the house a closed system, and a closed system has nowhere to put the water that expands every time the water heater heats a tank to 120 degrees. The pressure climbs with every cycle, and the rubber in the house takes the hit. The answer is an expansion tank on the water heater, a small tank with an air bladder that takes that extra water. If you have a PRV, and you almost certainly do, you have to have the tank. The expansion tank page covers the tank itself.
