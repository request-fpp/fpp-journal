A pressure reducing valve, the PRV, is the one part between the meter and your faucets that brings the city's water pressure down to what a house is built for. You feel it one of three ways: the water goes weak, the water goes wild, or the valve box in the flower bed fills with water. FPP Plumbing replaces PRVs from the Frisco office and the Plano office, with our own licensed plumbers, two on every shift. If you typed plumber near me because the shower goes thin after a minute or the pipes bang in the wall, the two numbers at the top belong to the Frisco office and the Plano office.

H2: What a Pressure Reducing Valve Does

A PRV, the water pressure regulator on your service line, takes the city's pressure on one side and gives the house a steady, lower pressure on the other. That's the whole job. The city sends water out at whatever pressure it takes to reach every house on the line: in most of our area that's 80 PSI and up, 100 in some places, 120, sometimes 140. The plumbing code caps the pressure inside a house at 80 PSI and requires a PRV wherever the city sends more, and here the city sends more. The people who make faucets, water heaters, dishwashers and washing machines say the same thing: the recommended working pressure for all of it is about 80 PSI and under. Above that, every joint, every valve, every rubber seal and the water heater's own fittings carry more pressure than they were meant to see day after day. Below about 40 the house feels it the other way: a thin stream in the shower.

We set a new valve to about 70 PSI. It's the middle we like: well under 80, so the system isn't under stress, and well over 40, so nobody stands under a trickle. In parts of McKinney and Frisco the city's own line runs at 50 to 60 PSI, the city has come out and measured it, and there a new PRV gives you what the main gives it.

The valve is the homeowner's. The city's responsibility ends at the meter; what the pressure does inside the house is yours to check, and a gauge on an outside spigot once a year is the check that catches a valve before it fails.

H2: A Water Pressure Test First: the Gauge on the Spigot

A pressure gauge screws onto an outside spigot or onto the washing machine bib. With no water running in the house it shows the static pressure. Over 80 is a problem whether anything feels wrong or not, and at 100 nothing feels wrong at all. Then open a tap inside and watch the needle. A healthy system drops a little and holds. A clogged PRV holds the number while nothing runs and gives it up the moment something does.

[Рядом фото 228: 100 PSI on the gauge at an outside spigot, Frisco]

That is the pressure test, and it is where every pressure call of ours starts: the static pressure in the system, then the pressure under load, with water running. It's also the test we ask you to run once a year. On a house that reads fine, you've spent two minutes. On a house at 110, you've found the reason the toilet fill valves keep failing. And if the PRV is not the problem, the gauge says so, and so do we.

H2: Signs a Pressure Reducing Valve Is Failing

Three things, and each one is a different failure inside the same valve.

The first and the most common: the pressure drops when you use water. The gauge reads fine, even high, with everything closed. Open the shower, and for the first twenty or thirty seconds it runs like it should, because that's the water that was sitting in the pipes under pressure. Then it falls off. Open a second tap and it falls off more. That valve is clogged. Hard water has filled it with scale, and it passes pressure but not volume.

The second is the opposite: too much pressure. Water sprays out of every faucet, the toilet fill valves stop shutting off and the tank keeps running, and when you close a tap you hear the pipe bang in the wall. That bang is water hammer, and it's a PRV that has stopped holding the city back.

The third is plain: the valve leaks in its box. You see a wet spot in the flower bed, or the city sends a notice about water use, or you lift the lid once and the box is full of water. The part people don't think about until it happens: the house's own shut-off valve sits in that same box. A valve that has stood in water is rusted when you need it: the night a pipe goes and you run out to close the house, the handle comes off in your hand.

H2: Can a Bad PRV Cause Low Water Pressure?

Yes, and it's the failure we see most. Hard water scales the valve shut from the inside a little more every year. Nothing bursts, nothing leaks, the house just gets weaker: the shower first, then the second bathroom when somebody runs the kitchen tap, then the sprinklers. The gauge on the spigot can still read 60 or 70 with nothing running, which is why the valve gets blamed last. One Frisco house read 35 to 40 PSI on the gauge with the valve clogged, and 75 the same day with the new one in. In Frisco we have measured 30 on a valve that was packed solid inside, and about 40 in McKinney on one the homeowner had tried to adjust himself.

Two other things read as low pressure here. The city itself: in a few areas of McKinney and Frisco the main carries 50 to 60 PSI, the city has measured it at the meter, and no valve makes more pressure than the city gives. And a valve nobody knows about: a second PRV in the same line, which is what the Plano story further down is about. The gauge, static and with a tap running, is what separates the three before anybody digs.

H2: How High Water Pressure Damages Plumbing

High pressure is quiet. At 100 PSI the shower feels great, and the house is wearing out early. The things that go first are the things with rubber in them: faucet cartridges, the fill valves in the toilets, the supply lines under the sinks, the washer hoses. Then the water heater and its connections, which their makers want fed at the recommended 80 PSI or under. Slab leaks are on that list too: a copper line with a thin spot in it opens sooner at 110 PSI than it would at 70.

On the Frisco page we tell where the newer houses leak first, at the valve box; on the McKinney page, what 108 PSI did to a shower valve. A plumber in Frisco and a plumber in McKinney keep finding the same thing: a house that felt fine and was over the line.

H2: How Long Does a Pressure Reducing Valve Last?

The usual figure is ten to fifteen years, and that's about right for a valve in a dry box with gravel under it. Here we see them fail from five. Two things shorten the life. The first is the box. A PRV is meant to sit in an accessible valve box with gravel on the bottom, so that rain and the sprinklers drain out instead of standing around the brass. When the builder buried it, or the ground moved and the box silted over, the valve sits in wet dirt and corrodes from the outside while the water scales it from the inside. The second is the water itself: hard, and it clogs the valve the way it clogs a shower head.

Most of the valves we replace are the builder's original. The house is ten years old or more, nobody has opened the box since the day it was set, and the first anybody hears of the PRV is the day it quits.

H2: Where the PRV Is Located in Frisco and Plano Homes

In most houses it's in the front flower bed, by the foundation, in a round or rectangular box with a lid, and the secondary shut-off valve for the house sits in the same box. Look for the lid first. If there's no lid in sight, the box has silted over, and finding it is part of our job. In many of the older Plano houses the box is gone under the dirt and the valve with it, and in many of those there was never a PRV at all, only the shut-off valve, as our plumber in Plano page shows.

In many of the houses built in the last ten years the builders bring the main line into the garage, and the PRV and the shut-off valve stand on the wall there, behind a small access panel. That is a better place for it. No dirt, no water, you can check it in a minute and we replace it without a dig. It still fails. Some at five years, some at ten, some at fifteen; the garage protects the outside of the valve, not the inside. The automatic shut-off valves that go in beside it now, a Moen Flo on the same line, have their own guide on this site.

H2: Can a PRV Be Adjusted, or Does It Need Replacement?

A PRV is adjusted with the bolt on top, turned until the gauge on the spigot reads what you want. We adjust a valve when it can take it: a valve that sits in a dry box or on a garage wall, not buried, not corroded, with no sign of wear or damage on it. That one gets set, and you pay for the test and the setting. H2: The Expansion Tank: What a PRV Does to the Water Heater

A PRV, in most cases, is also a one way valve. Water comes in, and nothing goes back out to the city. That makes the house a closed system, and a closed system has nowhere to put the water that expands every time the water heater heats a tank to 120 degrees. The pressure climbs with every cycle, and the rubber in the house takes the hit. The answer is an expansion tank on the water heater, a small tank with an air bladder that takes that extra water. If you have a PRV, you have to have the tank. On every PRV replacement the plumber checks the tank on the heater, and when a PRV goes into a house that never had one, the expansion tank goes in the same day.
