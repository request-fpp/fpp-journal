First Thing in a Plumbing Emergency: Shut the Water Off

Close the water first, then call. On most of these calls the pipe repair is the small part. What costs the money is the wet drywall and the floor, and that part grows every minute the water runs.

If one fixture is leaking, close the small stop valve at that fixture. Under a sink there are two on the wall, hot and cold. A toilet has one behind it, low, near the floor. Turn it to the right until it stops. If it's the water heater, close the valve on the cold line at the top of the tank. If you can't tell where the water is coming from, or the small valve does nothing, close the main shut-off valve for the whole house. The main shut-off valve guide has photos of where to look for that valve in a house here, and it shows how to close it.

Two things go wrong at this step. The small stops in a lot of houses here are builder grade, and one that hasn't been turned in ten years may not close at all, or it starts to leak the moment you touch it. Our guide to a leaking shut-off valve under the sink explains why. And a valve that's stuck should not be forced with a wrench: if it snaps, you have two leaks. Tell us on the phone that it won't turn, and we'll walk you to the next valve. If you ask, we text you the main valve guide while we talk. That doesn't work for everybody. It's hard to do on nerves, some valves need a tool, and some people just aren't handy. Say so, and the plumber closes it when he gets there.

A sewer backup is the other kind of emergency, and the rule there is different. Nothing is spraying, so there is nothing to close. Stop using water in the whole house, the washer and the dishwasher too, because every gallon you send down comes back up at the lowest drain.

What Counts as a Plumbing Emergency and What Can Wait Until Morning

It's an emergency when water is running and you can't stop it, or when sewage is coming up inside the house. Those calls go first, and we move our own schedule to get to them.

Call now for any of these: a pipe that burst, water coming through a ceiling, a water heater leaking in the attic, a main line backing up into the tub and the toilets, a shower that won't shut off, a house left with no water for the night.

These can wait until morning, and we'll tell you so on the phone:

  • A faucet that drips.
  • A slow sink drain, as long as the AC line isn't tied into it.
  • A toilet that runs. If the tank refills once every ten minutes, nothing bad is happening.
  • A water heater that isn't heating and isn't leaking. It's better looked at in daylight.
  • An outside spigot that drips at the spout or won't close all the way. It's a thin stream, and it runs outside. If a spigot leaks only while you're using it, stop using it: that water may be running inside the wall.
  • A faucet that won't shut off, once the valve under the sink or the main valve is closed. The water is stopped, and the faucet is a daytime job.
  • A garbage disposal that jammed or quit. That's garbage disposal repair on a weekday.

A call like that turns into a weekday visit: the $49 service call, and the $49 goes toward the repair. You don't pay an emergency fee for a job that waits until morning.

What Our Emergency Plumbers Found on Real Calls

Some of these are single jobs. Some are the same call, and it comes in every week.

A nail behind the baseboard. An evening call in Frisco. The homeowner could hear water running hard somewhere, and the tile floor in the bathroom had started to squish underfoot. We ran leak detection, took off the baseboard and opened the drywall. Behind it was a small copper manifold, fed by a 3/4 inch line that comes up from under the slab, and a nail had gone straight through that line. It went in when a contractor before us nailed on the baseboard, half a year or a year earlier. For all that time the nail itself plugged the hole. It sat in the water and rusted, and that evening it rusted through and the line let go.

The ceiling that came down in Plano. A daytime call from Plano: water was coming through the first floor ceiling, and part of the ceiling was already hanging down. This house had been through water once already. Over the winter the outside spigot had cracked, and it only leaked while somebody was using it, so every time they watered the yard, water was running inside the wall. Nobody saw it until the floor was wet and there was mold in the walls. That time we fixed the spigot, and our restoration partner opened the walls, took up the floor and went through the insurance claim with the homeowner. Spigots like that one are on the hose bib repair page.

Then the ceiling. We went upstairs and listened. Next to a sink on the second floor we could hear water running inside the wall. We opened the drywall where the sound was, and behind it was the hot water line that runs up to the water heater in the attic. It had a pinhole. A pinhole takes a long time to make: for months it only weeps, a drop at a time, and nothing shows. Around those same days the city had been working on the water lines in that neighborhood, and the water on the street went off and on a few times. Nobody did anything wrong there. But each time the pressure leaves a pipe and comes back, the pipe takes a small shock, and a pinhole that was half plugged with rust and calcium can open up. This one did. A drop became a stream, and the stream went through the ceiling below. We cut out that short piece of copper and put in new pipe. The drying is the restoration company's part. Finding a leak like this by sound is water leak detection.

A shower that won't shut off. This call comes in the evening, around eight, nine, ten o'clock. Somebody comes home from work, takes a shower before bed, and when he's done, the water won't stop. He turns the handle, the cartridge inside just spins, and the water keeps running. It's the hard water here. Calcium builds up inside the cartridge and the rubber wears out, and one day it doesn't close. You can't leave a shower running until morning. It runs up the water bill, and hot water running for hours finds the small cracks in a shower wall and gets behind the tile. On one of these calls the shower had been running for about an hour into a drain that was already half clogged. The drain couldn't keep up, and the water came over the edge. Close the main shut-off valve until we get there. The repair itself is a new cartridge, and that's faucet and shower valve repair.

The AC drain line, and a week with nobody home. This is still the call we get most. The condensate line from the air conditioner is tied into a sink drain. When that drain clogs, the water from the AC comes up out of the sink. With somebody home it's a wet cabinet. One homeowner was away on vacation for a week, and it ran over the sink for the whole week. He came back to a flooded house, and the cause was one clogged drain line under a sink. Opening that line is drain cleaning.

The main line, and the bathroom upstairs. When the main drain line is blocked, a toilet downstairs stops going down, and it looks like one clogged fixture. So the family uses the bathroom upstairs. Everything from the second floor runs to the lowest point in the house and comes out there, in the tub and through the downstairs toilets, and where a toilet flange is broken, from under the toilet. If one drain on the first floor backs up, don't send any more water down any drain in the house. A backup like that goes to the front of our schedule. A line that backs up again after it was cleared gets a camera in it, and that's sewer line repair and camera inspection.

The water heater in the attic. A water heater in an attic can leak for half a year before anybody knows. A little water goes into the drain pan under it, the ceiling stays dry, and nobody goes up there to look. Then one day the tank overheats and the crack opens up. Now it's an active leak. A drain pan is made for a drip. It can't carry a stream, so the water goes over the side and through the ceiling. A tank that's ten years old or more has a second problem. You go to close the valve above it, and the valve doesn't turn: ten years of rust and calcium. Then, when the supply lines are still in good shape, we close the main valve, take the heater off the line and connect the pipes straight through, around the tank. It's a temporary bypass. The house has cold water for the night, the tank gets drained so it stops dripping, and the new heater goes in on the nearest weekday. Putting in the new one is covered under water heater repair and replacement. The small expansion tank next to the heater can do the same thing: the copper it hangs on corrodes, or the tank was never connected right, and one day it lets go. In a garage that's a wet floor. In an attic it's a flood through the ceiling.

A recirculation pump that dripped all night. A Saturday morning call. The family woke up, and the ceiling on the second floor was wet. In the attic above it sits the water heater, and next to it a recirculation pump, the small pump that keeps hot water moving through the lines. The pump had cracked. It wasn't a hard leak. It dripped, steadily, all night, and by morning the ceiling was soaked. We took the old pump out, bought a new one and put it in.

The spigot somebody took out. An evening call: the garage floor was damp, the cabinets along the garage wall were wet inside, and outside, the brick on the front of the house was wet for ten or fifteen feet. We traced it into the wall. There used to be an outside spigot on that side of the house. Somebody took it out, cut the copper line off inside the wall, squeezed the end of the pipe flat and ran solder over it. That was the whole repair. The solder let go, and the line leaked inside the wall. We cut the pipe back and put a press cap on it.

After somebody else's work. Nine in the evening, and a family has no water in the house. That day a handyman, recommended by a friend, had come to change the outside spigot. The way he changed it broke the pipes inside the wall, and then he left. We opened the drywall and rebuilt what was broken, and the homeowner paid about three times what that spigot would have cost with a plumber on it from the start. Other calls of this kind: a nail through a PEX line in a closet wall, put there by somebody hanging shelves, and a manifold a previous plumber had built inside a wall out of push-fit fittings, which came apart and flooded the house. Plumbing in Texas is licensed work. Our jobs run under our Responsible Master Plumber license, M-44816, and the board that issues those licenses is the Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners.

Burst Pipes After a Hard Freeze: Where They Break

Nobody can tell you ahead of time which pipe a hard freeze will take. But pipe breaks in a freeze come from the same few places.

In a garage it's the supply line in the wall that feeds the outside spigot, when that wall has no insulation. In the attic it's any pipe that runs bare through thin insulation, and a burst pipe up there floods the whole house under it. In older houses it's the pipes in outside walls, where the insulation has thinned out over the years. And after a hard freeze we get more calls for leaks under the slab. The ground under a house should be warmer than the air, and we can't tell you why it happens. It does, and that work is slab leak repair.

One winter call: a pipe let go inside an outside wall and flooded in both directions, into the house and out through the brick. We opened the wall. The line was PEX put together with push-fit fittings, and one joint had pulled apart. We cut that part out and ran copper.

About half of these leaks don't show during the freeze. They show when everything starts to thaw and water gets back into the part of the pipe that split.

Before a freeze, let the faucets inside the house drip, and leave the cabinets under the sinks open. Leave the spigot outside alone: don't let it drip. A frost free spigot that was installed right doesn't freeze. What it needs is a cover. One that did freeze splits inside the wall and leaks only while it's in use, so nobody sees it for months. All summer we replace spigots that split the winter before: hose bib repair. And find the main shut-off valve before the cold comes.

Before It's an Emergency: Find the Main Shut-Off Valve

Every leak on this page gets smaller when somebody in the house can close the water in the first minute. So find your main shut-off valve this week, while nothing is leaking, and show it to everybody who lives in the house. If it's buried, or you can't find it, or it looks like it hasn't moved in ten years, that's worth a weekday visit. It's better to learn it on a weekday than on the night you need it.

Two more things do a lot of the work. Have the water pressure checked once a year. Over 80 PSI the whole system is under stress, and the weak part gives first, a cartridge or a supply line.

The other one is an automatic water shut-off valve. It sits on the main line and watches the flow. When a pipe bursts and water moves the way it never does in normal use, the valve sends a message to your phone, and depending on how it's set up, it closes the water by itself. With sensors on the floor it reacts to the first water it feels. In a house with a water heater or water lines in the attic, that's a very useful thing to have. Our guide to automatic water shut-off valves covers what goes into one.

When the Water Has Already Done Damage

The pipe is our part of the job. Drying the house is a different trade. Drywall and subfloor that stay wet grow mold inside the wall, where nobody looks. That's what happened in the Plano house above, the first time. The drying is done by a restoration partner we trust. They dry the walls and the floors, open what has to be opened, and deal with the insurance company. On an emergency we can come out together with their crew.

What your policy covers is between you and your insurer, and we won't promise anything about it. What you get from us is an invoice that says exactly what was repaired, and on the bigger jobs, photos and video of the work.

Emergency Plumbing FAQ

Do I pay the after hours fee if the repair turns out to be small? The emergency fee goes with the hour of the call, not with the size of the repair. It's named before the plumber heads out, so you know it before you decide. The repair has its own flat price, and you hear that price before any work starts. If the job can wait for a weekday, we tell you on that same call, and then it's the $49 service call.

The valve behind my toilet will not stop the water. What do I do? Close the main shut-off valve, and the water stops for the whole house. The little valves at toilets and sinks are often builder grade, and after years without being turned a lot of them don't close. With the main valve closed the toilet stops filling, and nothing else gets wet. The stop valve and the part inside the tank are a small job, and it's on the toilet repair page.

Water is coming through my ceiling. Where is it coming from? Not always from the room right above the wet spot. Water runs along the framing and the pipes before it drops. On the Plano call above, the ceiling came down on the first floor, and the leak was inside a wall on the second floor, on the line that goes up to the water heater in the attic. Close the main shut-off valve first, so the ceiling stops taking water. Finding the spot is our part of the job.

My shower will not turn off. What do I do until the plumber gets here? Close the main shut-off valve for the house. A shower has no small valve of its own that you can reach, so the main valve is the only way to stop it. Don't keep forcing the handle: the cartridge inside is already broken. With the water off nothing is getting worse, but the house has no water until the cartridge is replaced. Call, and you hear the arrival window and the emergency fee for that hour.

Can you stop the leak tonight and do the full repair later? Yes, when the full repair isn't night work. A water heater is the usual case. We take the leaking heater off the line, connect the pipes around it when they're in good shape, drain the tank, and set the new heater for the nearest weekday, with a city permit where the city requires one. A slab leak waits for daytime too. A burst pipe we can reach gets repaired that night.

Do you come out on Sundays and holidays? Yes. Emergency plumbing runs 24/7, weekends and holidays included, with a licensed plumber on duty. The office keeps weekday hours, 8 AM to 5 PM, but the phones don't follow the office. On a Sunday or a holiday the emergency fee applies, and you hear the amount before anybody drives out.
