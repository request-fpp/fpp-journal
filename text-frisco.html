Plumbing in Frisco Homes: Newer Streets, Older Streets, Different Calls

Frisco is a young city, and most of the houses we work in are under twenty years old. That decides what breaks, and what a Frisco plumber gets called for. In the newer communities, Fields, Panther Creek, The Grove, Phillips Creek Ranch, Starwood, the house is fine and the small parts are not: the builder's pressure reducing valve, the faucet cartridges, the expansion tank on the water heater, the outside spigots. Closer to downtown and along Preston Road, where homes went up before 2005, it's age: water heaters past their tenth year, drains with years of buildup, and now and then copper under the slab.

The Plumbing Calls We Get Most in Frisco

These are the plumbing services Frisco homeowners call us for most. Each one has its own page.

A sink that won't drain, with the AC line tied in

This is the top call right now, here like everywhere else we work. The AC condensate line is tied into the sink drain under the cabinet, and when that drain clogs, the sink fills and water runs into the cabinet. In the photo the AC line is the black one, marked, coming into the drain above the trap, and the cable is in that drain line clearing it. One house in Frisco has this setup in the girls' bathroom, and we have cleared that sink twice in a year and a half. Hair goes down that drain, and a lot of organic buildup from soap and face wash goes with it. Calcium builds up in there too. The AC line adds its own: dirt and mold grow inside it, and the water it drips pushes all of that into the same drain. A drain like that fills up again with time, and we tell you so before we start. Drain cleaning has the details.

The pressure reducing valve (PRV): too high or too low

The PRV sits in a box at the front of the house, between the meter and the house. We replace them all the time, for three reasons: hard water scales them up, they wear out with age, and the builder's box is often full of dirt, so nobody sees the valve until it fails. When a PRV fails high, pressure climbs over 80 PSI and starts eating cartridges, fill valves and the water heater. When it fails low, the shower fades. A gauge on the hose bib tells us in a minute. And the third way: the valve itself starts to leak in its box. The clip here is a PRV leaking in its box, water running out of the old valve into the mud. We dug it out and put in a new valve with a new shut-off valve next to it. The PRV replacement page shows how one is changed.

Water heaters, garage or attic, and the expansion tank

Another call we get all the time. Most of them fail from age and from never being touched: nobody flushes the tank, the anode rod is never changed, the expansion tank is never checked. We say this without blame, because nobody tells a homeowner to do it. The expansion tank is the quiet one. There is a rubber bladder inside, it cracks with time, water fills the tank, the tank rusts, and one day it drips onto the heater. A scaled tank also drops your hot water pressure. Water heater repair and replacement and expansion tank replacement are separate pages, because they are separate jobs.

Outside spigots that crack or won't shut off

The spigot on your wall is a frost free one. If the builder and the plumber installed it right, it should not freeze or burst. It still needs a cover before a freeze, and that is what our reminders say: let the faucets inside the house drip, put covers on the outside spigots, find the main shut-off valve before you need it. It helps. And still, every spring and half the summer we replace outdoor spigots that cracked inside the wall during the freeze. The crack shows nothing until the first time you turn the hose on, and then water runs into the wall. Others simply leak: you close the handle and the water keeps running. Nothing burst, it just will not shut off. Both kinds are on the hose bib repair page, and the guide on the main shut-off valve shows how to stop the water fast if a pipe does burst.

Main sewer lines with roots, and backups that come back

The soil here moves. Joints in the sewer line open a little, roots find the water, and a year later the toilets back up. We clear the roots with the chain snake and run a camera through the line. Sometimes the camera goes in first, when the line is clogged but still lets it through. Sometimes it goes in after the clearing, because in a packed line the camera shows nothing. It depends on how bad the clog is. If the camera shows a separated joint, we repair that section. That repair is on the sewer line repair and camera inspection page. A backup that comes back after clearing is the sign.

Dripping faucets, cartridges and tub spouts

Hard water, same as the rest of Texas. Calcium builds up inside the cartridge and breaks down the rubber, and the faucet drips or won't shut off. One we pulled came out crusted white, it is in the photo here. The tub spout goes the same way. The diverter inside it, the part that sends the water up to the shower head when you pull the knob, scales up, its rubber and plastic wear through, and then water runs from the spout and the shower head at the same time. Faucet and shower valve repair is its own page.

Garbage disposals that jam or leak

In the newer homes the builder puts in the most basic unit there is: a third or half a horsepower, with a one year warranty from the manufacturer. It handles heavy food scraps badly, it jams and clogs, and in two or three years it stops or starts to leak from the bottom. A disposal like that is not worth repairing, it gets replaced. We put in Moen 3/4 horsepower units with a ten year manufacturer warranty. The garbage disposal repair page has more on that.

Water Pressure in Frisco Homes: What the Gauge Shows

We put a gauge on the hose bib at a lot of houses here. Very often it reads over 80 PSI, and 90 is not rare. In a few homes we have seen 105. Above 80 the whole system is under stress, so when we put in a new PRV, we usually set it to 70 PSI. Then there is the other end. With hard water an old PRV rusts and clogs inside until almost nothing gets through, and the gauge shows 30 PSI. Too high or too low, it is usually the same valve. Check your pressure once a year.

Where Newer Houses Leak First: The Valve Box

In the newer homes here, when the main water line leaks, it is usually at one spot: around the pressure reducing valve and the secondary shut-off valve next to it. In about half the houses nobody has ever serviced them, the valve box is buried and packed with wet dirt, and in that dirt the pipe corrodes, pinholes show up, and one day it rusts through. The valves and the pipe should sit in air, not in wet dirt: a clean valve box has no dirt in it. That is the main trend we see here right now. Water line repair is the page for that leak.

Slab leaks are rare in Frisco next to Plano, where the houses are twenty years older. Frisco is one of the newest areas in the North Dallas suburbs, and in all our time here we have repaired maybe ten slab leaks. We fix them the same way as anywhere, with brazing and a pressure test before the hole is closed, and slab leak repair has the details.

The Nail in the Wall: The Leak Nobody Can Find

In the newer homes we keep finding the same hidden leak, and it was made when the house was built: a shelf nail driven through a water line behind the drywall, most often PEX, that plugs its own hole for a year or two and then rusts through. Water leak detection has the details on how a leak like that gets found.

We had exactly that case in a Frisco garage. Somebody had hung a shelf, and a nail went through the pipe in the wall. A year later the nail rusted through and the pipe began to seep, two or three drops a minute. The wall backs onto a closet, and the closet is where it showed: the baseboard started to mold, the floor was wet, and it went on for half a year while nobody could tell where the water came from. We found the leak from the garage side, opened the drywall, cut out the damaged piece and put in new pipe. The hole is in the second photo. That is the hardest kind of leak to find, because there is almost no water.

What a Freeze Breaks

A freeze breaks more than outside spigots here. In garages we very often see the water line that feeds the outside spigot burst, because a garage is cold no matter what. In one of those garages, the one in the photo, the line to the spigot had burst inside the wall and was flooding the garage. We came out, shut the water off, cut out the burst section and put in new copper.

Some lines run inside the walls of the house too, and when the wall is not insulated well, or the pipe is not fully insulated, the pipe bursts inside the wall. Then we open the drywall, cut out the burst section, put in new pipe and insulate it the right way. By then it is a long story: a flood and an insurance claim. Our restoration partner dries the house and does the mitigation, so mold does not spread. What cracks inside a spigot is in our post on outside faucets after a freeze.

Backflow Preventers on Sprinkler Systems

We replace backflow preventers on sprinkler systems often. Most of them simply rust. The handles and the shut-off valves on the backflow rust until they will not turn, and then you cannot close the water. After the backflow the line is usually PVC, and in the ground that plastic is brittle: the ground moves or a root presses on it, and the pipe cracks.

An old backflow with rusted valves also cannot be tested, and very often it has to be replaced after that. This is a plumber's job. We pull the city permit, put in the new backflow and pass the city inspection. Once the new assembly is in, it gets tested and the test report goes to the city.

From the Job: Four Calls Out of This Office

The buried PRV. A homeowner called about a water bill that had been high for a couple of months. Nothing in the house was leaking. In the yard, the pressure reducing valve was buried in a box full of dirt, and a pinhole in it had been running for months, slowly growing, with nowhere to show until a wet spot came up on the lawn. We dug it out and found the leak. We replaced the PRV and the shut-off valve next to it, took out the dirt, put gravel under the valves for drainage, set both in new valve boxes with covers, and put unions on both sides, so the next time a PRV has to go, it comes out in minutes instead of a dig. Leak detection is the page for a bill that climbs with nothing wet inside.

The main line that was more than roots. This one started in January, in the freeze. A family's sewer backed up with snow on the ground, and we came out to clear it. Under the snow we could not find the cleanouts, so we pulled a toilet and ran the snake and the camera through the flange. The camera showed a line packed with roots, and the pipe at that spot was cracked, separated and shifted. The chain snake took the roots out, a pile of them, and the house drained again. The family kept our report, so they knew the pipe was broken. They set a budget, planned it, and called us back to repair it, so it would not happen again. The separation was right where the line leaves the foundation: the pipe comes out of the slab, hits a 22.5-degree fitting, and drops steeply. To reach that joint we dug five to seven feet down next to the foundation, replaced the separated section, put the line back together and ran water through it on camera to prove it drains. Roots come back where a joint is open. A repaired joint stays closed.

A week with the plunger. A Frisco family called about a toilet that would not flush right. For a week they had been using the plunger after every flush: it clogged, they plunged it, it worked, and the next flush with paper clogged it again. We ran a six-foot toilet auger through it and the line opened, but the plumber felt something was still not right. So we pulled the toilet. The bolts were rusted, the wax ring was ruined, and there was already water and waste on the tile under it. Then the camera went in, and right at the first bend sat a plastic sponge, the head of a toilet cleaning wand. Somebody had flushed it during a cleaning, most likely. We sent in a short pickup tool and got it out. That does not always work. This time it did. Toilet repair is the page for a toilet that keeps clogging.

The expansion tank that hung on the pipe. A homeowner called about a drip at the water heater: a supply line had started to drip. When we got there, the expansion tank was hanging on the PEX line with nothing else holding it. On the day it was installed that worked. A tank full of air weighs almost nothing, and the pipe held it fine. But the rubber bladder inside fails in three to five years. This one had failed, and the tank was full of water. It is a two gallon tank, so two gallons of water hung on a plastic pipe and bent it. A tank like that rusts through and leaks sooner or later. Here the pipe would have given out first and flooded the house. We replaced the tank and secured the new one the way the code requires. The job itself is on the expansion tank replacement page.

Frisco Plumbing Questions

Do you pull permits with the City of Frisco? Yes, when the job requires one. FPP Plumbing is registered with the City of Frisco as a plumbing contractor. Water heater replacements and sewer repairs usually need a city permit and a city inspection, small repairs don't. We tell you which one yours is before we start.

Do you work on newer homes in Frisco? Yes. In the newest neighborhoods, where houses are under ten years old, a lot of our work is builder grade PRVs, cartridges, expansion tanks, and water heaters installed with the bare minimum.

Is Frisco water hard? Yes, like the rest of Texas. Calcium builds up in faucet cartridges, toilet fill valves and water heaters, and it is behind most of the dripping and the low hot water pressure we see here. Flushing the water heater once a year helps.

What should I do before a freeze in Frisco? Let the faucets inside the house drip, put covers on the outside spigots, and know where the main shut-off valve is before the night it drops below twenty. If a pipe bursts, close the main first, then call. After the freeze, run every outside spigot once: a crack inside the wall shows up only when the hose is on.

Who answers the Frisco line after hours? The same people. Both office lines ring to everyone on duty, and whoever is closest to you comes out, a licensed plumber, not an answering service. When do I pay, and does paying by card cost extra? When the job is done. Zelle, a check or cash costs you nothing extra. A card payment has a processing fee, and you hear about it before you pay. A larger job can also be split into payments through PayPal or Klarna, when they approve it. The amount is the flat price you agreed to before we started.
