24/7 Emergency Plumbing: How a Call Works

Day or night, a call goes the same way. You don't have to decide first if you need an emergency plumber or a regular one. Call, tell us what's going on, and we sort it out on the phone.

From the Job

Real calls, written down the way they happened.

Water coming out of the foundation. A homeowner got a big water bill, and then a notice from the city about it. We ran leak detection, found roughly where the leak was, and started the access hole outside. We had barely started digging when water was already pushing out from under the foundation. The copper line went through the concrete with no sleeve, and right at that spot a pinhole had turned into a serious leak. The pipe past it was in bad shape too, so we dug a tunnel until we reached sound copper, tied in there, and ran new pipe, brazed in the tunnel. The new line comes out under the foundation, not through it. Permit pulled, city inspection passed. Slab leak repair has the details.

The most common call right now: a sink drain with the AC line tied in. Some days we get up to five of these calls. The sink line collects soap, hair, grease, calcium and dirt, the AC condensate line feeds it water all day, and when the line clogs, the sink fills up and water comes out into the cabinet. If you see water inside the cabinet and the faucet is closed, look for a small pipe tied in near the trap: that's the AC line. We clear it and tell you up front what to expect with that line. The drain cleaning page has the details.

A night call that could have been a morning call. Water running in the house, the homeowner can't stop it, come now. We come out, and the water is running from a faucet into the sink and going down the drain. The cartridge had seized open, and the shut-off valves under the sink were so old they would not close. No leak, no flood. We closed the main shut-off valve at the house and replaced the two valves under the sink; the faucet, old and not worth repairing, is a daytime job. If you know where your main shut-off valve is, a night like this turns into a morning appointment. Our guide shows where it is and how to close it.

One clog, the whole house. Late evening: the toilets won't flush, and every flush comes back up in the tub and the shower. The family thought the downstairs toilet was the problem, so they kept using the upstairs bathroom and flushed a few more times. All of it came back down. The main sewer line was blocked past the outside cleanout, and a house has one drain system: upstairs still flushes only because there is room left in the pipes, and everything comes out at the lowest point downstairs. When they called, we said stop: no toilets, no water at all. But the washer and the dishwasher were still running, draining into the same blocked line, and that water had nowhere to go but the shower pan and the floor. Dirty water through the house. An older toilet adds one more weak spot: a worn wax ring does not hold that pressure, and sewage comes out between the toilet and the floor. We cleared the main line through the cleanout, and the restoration crew came in behind us to dry the house and handle the insurance side. The rule is simple: if one drain backs up, stop every drain in the house, including the washer and the dishwasher, and call. Drain cleaning has the details.

Chewed through in the attic. Mice or squirrels had chewed a PEX water line in the attic, in two spots at once, and water was coming down through the ceiling and the walls. The homeowner was in a panic. We tried to walk him to the main shut-off valve on the phone, and it did not work. That night we had a truck free, so we got there fast, closed the water, replaced the chewed sections, and brought our restoration partner's crew with us, so the drying started the same night. That is what emergency plumbing is for, and we're glad we could do it. But every homeowner should know two things before a night like this: where the main shut-off valve is and how to close it. Our guide shows both, and which tool to keep by the door.

Do I get photos or a report of the work? You get an invoice that says what we did. Do you handle the water damage too? We stop the leak and fix the plumbing. For drying out the walls and floors we work with a restoration partner we trust, and on an emergency their crew can come out together with us. Call or text us about it, one call covers both.

What payment methods do you accept? All the usual ways: credit and debit cards, Apple Pay, PayPal, Zelle, Venmo, checks and cash, and we can take crypto too.
